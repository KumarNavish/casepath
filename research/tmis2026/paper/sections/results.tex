\subsection{Complete request lists: safer with the cost-efficient model, more deferrals with both (H1, H2)}
\label{sec:results-tenancy}

On the tenancy claims, executing the rules was at least as safe as letting the model apply them, and safer with the cost-efficient model, but it requested fewer required documents at once (Table~\ref{tab:tenancy}, Figure~\ref{fig:tenancy}). With \texttt{gpt-6-luna}, \casepath with exact deferral made \tenLunaCPxUnsafe{} unsafe errors per claim against \tenLunaBoneUnsafe{} for \design{Rules in context}: no unnecessary request in any run (\design{Rules in context}: \pct{\tenLunaBoneUnnec} of requests) and \pct{\tenLunaCPxMiss} silent misses against \pct{\tenLunaBoneMiss}. With \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}, both designs were almost error-free (\tenTerraCPxUnsafe{} and \tenTerraBoneUnsafe{} unsafe errors per claim). H1 is therefore supported for the cost-efficient model and shows no difference for the frontier model.

With both models, \casepath held more required documents behind questions (H2). \design{Rules in context} requested \pct{\tenTerraBoneRecall} (\texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}) and \pct{\tenLunaBoneRecall} (\texttt{gpt-6-luna}) of required documents at once; \casepath with exact deferral requested \pct{\tenTerraCPxRecall} and \pct{\tenLunaCPxRecall} and deferred \pct{\tenTerraCPxDefer} and \pct{\tenLunaCPxDefer}. The production engine's strong Kleene semantics deferred far more (\pct{\tenTerraCPkDefer} and \pct{\tenLunaCPkDefer}); exact deferral reduced deferrals by \tenTerraExactCut\% and \tenLunaExactCut\% and added no unnecessary request, but, contrary to the second part of H2, it added \tenTerraExactAddedMiss{} and \tenLunaExactAddedMiss{} silent misses among \tenTerraExactNR{} required documents (three runs): supplied documents that \casepath had judged sufficient and the reference marks as insufficient or missing. The production engine had hidden these judgment errors by holding the documents; Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} guarantees exactness with respect to the judgments, not their correctness. \design{Model executes rules}, which received the same three-valued semantics as text, fell in between (\pct{\tenTerraBtwoRecall} requested, \pct{\tenTerraBtwoDefer} deferred): instructed to apply strong Kleene logic, the model reproduced part of its incompleteness.

\paragraph{Why \casepath defers}
Most of \casepath's deferrals stem from one fact the messages do not state. With \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}, \defRouting{} of the \defTotal{} required documents it held (\defRoutingPct\%) waited for the answer to whether a termination notice or rent-increase claim had been received, and with \texttt{gpt-6-luna} \defLunaRouting{} of \defLunaTotal{} (\defLunaRoutingPct\%); the reference generator presumes this routing fact, while \casepath's interpretation policy treats an unstated fact as open. With \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}, another \defClaim{} were held because the verifier did not admit the dispute family, which hands the claim to a person, and only \defOther{} depended on other conditions. When the routing fact is presumed once the family is admitted---a policy change evaluated post hoc on the same judgments---\casepath requests \pct{\presRecall} and \pct{\presLunaRecall} of the required documents of admitted claims, with \pct{\presUnnec} and \pct{\presLunaUnnec} unnecessary requests, close to \design{Rules in context}.

\subsection{Changed facts: execution helps with the cost-efficient model (H3)}
\label{sec:results-changed}

When one fact of a theft case changed, the result depended on the model (Table~\ref{tab:theft}, Figure~\ref{fig:theft}). With \texttt{gpt-6-luna}, \casepath made \thLunaCPWrong{} wrong and \thLunaCPMissed{} missed changes over the 81 pair runs ($F_1$ \thLunaCPFone{} [\thLunaCPFoneLo, \thLunaCPFoneHi]), while \design{Rules in context} made \thLunaRWrong{} wrong and \thLunaRMissed{} missed changes ($F_1$ \thLunaRFone{} [\thLunaRFoneLo, \thLunaRFoneHi]); majority voting over three runs did not close the gap ($F_1$ \thLunaRscFone). With \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}, the order reversed: \design{Rules in context} made \thTerraRWrong{} wrong and \thTerraRMissed{} missed changes ($F_1$ \thTerraRFone{} [\thTerraRFoneLo, \thTerraRFoneHi]) against \casepath's \thTerraCPWrong{} and \thTerraCPMissed{} ($F_1$ \thTerraCPFone{} [\thTerraCPFoneLo, \thTerraCPFoneHi]); \design{Direct} made \thTerraDWrong{} and \thTerraDMissed{}. H3 is supported for the cost-efficient model only. Restricting the scoring to the seven conditions whose references the independent re-derivation reproduced does not change any ranking.

The original study had found the same advantage as \texttt{gpt-6-luna}: with \texttt{gpt-5.6-terra}, the extracted rules in context produced 41 wrong changes against \casepath's 15 in one run of the same 27 pairs, mostly documents that disappeared from one of two independently generated checklists. \casepath's errors varied much less across the three models (0.21 to 0.56 wrong changes per pair) than those of \design{Rules in context} (0.27 to 1.52), because the model's role in \casepath is confined to judging conditions.

\subsection{Unresolved facts: no premature requests, fewer side effects with execution (H4)}
\label{sec:results-unresolved}

When a condition was left open, almost no design requested its documents prematurely: \casepath never did, and the in-context designs did so in at most \thLunaRPremature\% of unresolved-fact pairs. The guarantee of Proposition~\ref{prop:sound} was thus largely matched by the models themselves. The designs differed in what else changed. With \texttt{gpt-6-luna}, \casepath left every other request unchanged in \thLunaCPStable\% of pairs and \design{Rules in context} in \thLunaRStable\%; with \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}, \design{Rules in context} did so in \thTerraRStable\% and \casepath in \thTerraCPStable\%. H4 is supported for the cost-efficient model only; \casepath's remaining changes have a single cause, traced in Section~\ref{sec:results-origin}.

\subsection{Consistency across runs (H5)}
\label{sec:results-stability}

On the tenancy claims, \design{Rules in context} was more consistent across runs than \casepath for both models, so H5 is not supported (differences in mean test--retest agreement of \ptTerraHFiveDiff{} and \ptLunaHFiveDiff{} for \casepath). Execution is deterministic, but its inputs are not: \casepath's two model calls each vary between runs, and the verifier's decisions vary most. On the theft cases the picture depended on the model: with \texttt{gpt-6-luna}, \casepath's request lists agreed more across runs than those of \design{Rules in context} (\thLunaCPRetest{} versus \thLunaRRetest); with \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}, less (\thTerraCPRetest{} versus \thTerraRRetest).

\subsection{Why: judgments, verification and semantics}
\label{sec:results-mechanism}

Three mechanisms explain these results. First, \casepath's interpreter almost never made a confident error. With \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}, of \jaTotal{} condition judgments on claims whose family it identified, \jaCorrect{} matched the reference, \jaFlipped{} contradicted it and \jaUnres{} were unresolved. Of the unresolved ones, \jaUnresFalse{} concerned facts that are false in the scenario but never mentioned in the message: the reference reads silence as false, \casepath's policy reads it as open. The remaining \jaUnresTrue{} concerned true facts the message did not state clearly enough, chiefly the routing fact above.

Second, the value of independent verification depended on the model. With \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}, the verifier rejected \jaRejCorrect{} condition verdicts, all of them correct, and accepted all \jaFlipped{} wrong ones, so each rejection turned a correct verdict into an open question; without it, \casepath requested \pct{\tenTerraCPxnRecall} instead of \pct{\tenTerraCPxRecall} of required documents with the same unsafe-error rate, and it made fewer wrong changes on the theft cases (\thTerraCPnWrong{} versus \thTerraCPWrong). With \texttt{gpt-6-luna}, the verifier reduced wrong theft changes from \thLunaCPnWrong{} to \thLunaCPWrong{} but again increased tenancy deferrals.

Third, the semantics determine what an unresolved judgment becomes. Executing the same rules on \casepath's recorded judgments with unresolved facts read as false turned its deferrals into silent misses (\pct{\tvTerraClosedMiss} and \pct{\tvLunaClosedMiss} of required tenancy documents); reading them as true turned them into unnecessary requests (\pct{\tvTerraEagerUnnec} and \pct{\tvLunaEagerUnnec} of requests) and, on the theft cases, into premature requests in about half of the unresolved-fact pairs (\tvThTerraEagerPremature\% and \tvThLunaEagerPremature\%). Deferral is therefore not an avoidable inefficiency of three-valued execution: it is what remains when neither guess is safe. More generally, Figure~\ref{fig:propagation} shows the exact expected errors over all \propClaims{} tenancy claims when each condition is misjudged with probability 0.2, computed over every partial assignment of their flags with the production engine. If errors take the form ``unresolved'', exact deferral turns all of them into deferrals and none into unsafe errors; strong Kleene evaluation defers \propKleeneExtra\% more documents than necessary; reading open facts as false turns them into silent misses, and reading them as true into unnecessary requests. If errors are confident wrong verdicts, all four semantics fail alike. Three-valued execution is therefore safe exactly to the extent that the model expresses uncertainty instead of guessing, as \casepath's interpreter did in \jaUnres{} of its \the\numexpr\jaUnres+\jaFlipped\relax{} non-matching judgments.

\subsection{Where the remaining errors come from}
\label{sec:results-origin}

Every request \casepath makes carries a trace to the rule and the case quotation behind it, which locates its errors exactly. With \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}, \ecWrongFaulty{} of its \thTerraCPWrong{} wrong theft changes and \ecMissedFaulty{} of its \thTerraCPMissed{} misses trace to five of the 98 automatically extracted rules: a route that demands a receipt and an expert valuation where the sources accept either, a route that adds a bank statement, an unconditional claim-form section, and two conditions broader than their sources. The same five rules produced the errors of the original study. The model applying the rules in context saw the same faulty rules but, reading them beside the sources, avoided most of their consequences when it was the frontier model. A blind verification of every extracted rule against its cited passages flagged all \rvImplicatedFlagged{} faulty rules, but also \the\numexpr\rvFlagged-\rvImplicatedFlagged\relax{} of the other \the\numexpr\rvRules-\rvImplicated\relax{}, so it can direct expert review but cannot replace it. On the tenancy claims, whose rules are correct by construction, \casepath's \tenTerraCPxFP{} unnecessary requests follow from the \jaFlipped{} wrong verdicts and its \tenTerraCPxMissN{} silent misses are supplied files it judged sufficient that the reference marks as insufficient.

Citations barely distinguished the designs: every quotation that \design{Rules in context}, \design{Model executes rules} and \casepath's interpreter produced with \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra} occurred verbatim in its source (\citBone, \citBtwo{} and \citCPProp{} quotations); with \texttt{gpt-6-luna}, \pct{\citLunaBoneExact} of the quotations of \design{Rules in context} and \pct{\citLunaCPPropExact} of the interpreter's did, and \casepath's admission rejects the judgments whose quotations do not. The difference lies in what a citation supports: \casepath's quotations support a recorded judgment of a named condition, from which each request follows by a rule; a model-written plan's quotations support its stated reason, whose consistency with the rules is not checked.

\subsection{Cost and runtime}
\label{sec:results-cost}

\casepath costs more per case than letting the same model apply the rules, because it makes two model calls, but the model matters more than the design. With prompt caching, a tenancy claim cost $\$\costTerraTenCP\times10^{-3}$ with \casepath and $\$\costTerraTenBone\times10^{-3}$ with \design{Rules in context} on \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra}, and $\$\costLunaTenCP\times10^{-3}$ and $\$\costLunaTenBone\times10^{-3}$ on \texttt{gpt-6-luna}; a theft case cost $\$\costTerraThCP\times10^{-3}$ and $\$\costTerraThR\times10^{-3}$ on \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra} and $\$\costLunaThCP\times10^{-3}$ and $\$\costLunaThR\times10^{-3}$ on \texttt{gpt-6-luna}. \casepath with \texttt{gpt-6-luna} was thus at least as accurate on the theft cases as the frontier model applying the rules in context ($F_1$ \thLunaCPFone{} versus \thTerraRFone), at half its cost per case. The deterministic part is negligible: the production engine evaluates a 100-step synthetic process in \scaleHundredMs{}\,ms, exact deferral over ten open flags takes \scaleExactTen{}\,s, and real claims have at most five flags. All new experiments, including pilots and diagnostics, cost $\$\spendTerra$ with \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra} and $\$\spendLuna$ with \texttt{gpt-6-luna}.
