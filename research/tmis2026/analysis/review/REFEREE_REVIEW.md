# Referee report

**Manuscript:** "CasePath: Agents That Read, Rules That Decide. Requesting Evidence for Insurance Claims with Executable Workflows" (Kumar, Hoesli, Javadov, Ollier), submitted to ACM TMIS. 65 pages: 20 pages of body and 45 pages of references and appendices A–I.

Page numbers refer to the submitted PDF. Quotations come from the PDF text.

---

## (a) Summary for the editor

1. The submission presents CasePath, an LLM-based system that prepares legal-protection insurance claims. Several independent model "readings" extract each workflow fact with a verbatim quotation. Code admits a value only when all readings agree and the quotation is found, fills unstated facts with source-backed presumptions, and evaluates the organisation's workflow over every completion of the open facts ("exact deferral"). Documents whose need depends on an open fact are held behind a question.
2. A short formal section (four propositions) shows that strong Kleene evaluation over-holds when workflow branches reconverge, and that supervaluation-style evaluation is sound relative to the admitted facts.
3. Two earlier pre-registered studies, reported in full, failed. A third, confirmatory test on sealed synthetic data (96 tenancy claims, 72 theft pairs) under a fixed-sequence plan confirmed three hypotheses on a cost-efficient model. CasePath beat the same model applying the rules in one prompt on theft and tenancy, and beat a frontier single prompt on tenancy. It failed the fourth hypothesis, against Self-consistency on theft, so the fifth was not tested.
4. The tenancy advantage is concentrated in 33 author-designed claims whose defining event is denied or left open. On the 63 ordinary claims there is no difference from the frontier single prompt. The tenancy references are generated by the same engine and silence policy that CasePath implements.
5. The paper also describes a registered but not yet conducted study with five claims experts on three real files. It releases code, data, cached responses and analysis scripts.

## (b) Recommendation: major revision

Three reasons decide it:

1. **The evidence is honest but narrower than the framing.**
   - The confirmed tenancy effect rests on references produced by CasePath's own engine and silence policy.
   - The effect sits almost entirely in 33 gating claims that the authors added to the test design. A probe written with knowledge of that design preceded them.
   - The per-document error measure multiplies a single gating misjudgement into five to seven errors.
   - The abstract, introduction and conclusion do not carry these qualifications.
2. **The information-systems contribution is underdeveloped.**
   - There is no design-science framing, and no managerial or governance analysis (rule authoring and maintenance, accountability, audit).
   - Two of the four stated contributions are not results: a release, and an expert study that has not run.
   - The related work omits lines of work a TMIS reader expects.
3. **Several soundness questions can be resolved with data the authors already hold, but must be resolved before acceptance:**
   - inference with 9 theft clusters, and a crossed story structure that the clustering ignores;
   - the asymmetry in development effort between the product and the frozen baselines;
   - the cache-shared checklist-only comparison and an inconsistent cost split;
   - the scope conditions of the propositions;
   - the independence of the AI agent that wrote the test texts.

None of these is fatal. The study is unusually transparent, and the failed hypothesis is reported straight. A revision that rescopes the claims and adds the analyses below could be publishable.

## (c) Major concerns

### M1. The contribution and positioning for an IS audience are not clear on page 1, and two of the four contributions are not contributions

**Where:** Abstract and Section 1 (pp. 1–2); Section 2 (pp. 2–3); Table 1 (p. 4).

**What:**
- The first page tells the reader what CasePath does and gives four numbers. It does not say what an IS reader learns that generalises beyond this product: a design principle, a theory of when to separate reading from deciding, or an organisational implication.
- The keywords list "design science", but the paper never positions itself within design science research. It cites no DSR framework, and there is no explicit statement of the problem class, design principles, boundary conditions, or the evaluation's role in a build–evaluate cycle. Section 5 ("How the design was found") is exactly such a cycle, and it is left unframed.
- Contribution (2), the release, and contribution (4), "A registered study in which claims experts review CasePath's checklists on real cases", are not research results. Contribution (1) is stated as "with proofs", but the proofs are elementary consequences of Kleene monotonicity and of the definition of supervaluation (Appendix A, pp. 22–23). The novelty lies in applying certain-answer semantics to workflow-gated document needs, and in the reconvergence example (Prop. 3.2).
- Table 1 is self-serving:
  - Column 5 ("learns from corrections") marks CasePath "yes (§4)". Yet Study 2's evolution experiment found no separation from Rules in context with corrections as memory (p. 10, "1.173 against 1.432 … p = 0.297"), and Knowledge did not enter a single scored checklist in the confirmatory test (p. 15).
  - Column 6 marks CasePath "registered (§7)", which is not a property of a system.
  - Most prior work is marked "n.s." on criteria the prior work never set out to meet.

**Why it matters:** TMIS publishes work that yields design knowledge or managerial insight for information systems in organisations. As written, the paper reads as a system report with a benchmark.

**What would resolve it:**
- Rewrite the first two paragraphs of the introduction to state the problem class: which evidence to request when a decision depends on facts the file does not settle.
- State the artefact, and the generalisable design knowledge. The four "Design principles" on p. 19 are a good start; give each its boundary condition and the evidence behind it.
- Frame Sections 5–6 explicitly as DSR evaluation episodes.
- Reduce the contributions to (1) the decision rule and its properties, (2) the artefact, and (3) the confirmatory evaluation. Mention the release and the expert-study protocol as resources, not contributions.
- In Table 1, drop columns 5 and 6, or code them only where CasePath has evidence.
- Add a short subsection on organisational implications: who authors and reviews workflows and presumptions, and what that costs; accountability when a presumption is wrong; auditability; and how holds and questions change the handler's work.

**Missing related work** that a TMIS referee would expect. All of these exist; where I am unsure of a detail I describe the line rather than cite it.

- **Design science research in IS:**

  - Gregor and Hevner (2013), "Positioning and Presenting Design Science Research for Maximum Impact", MIS Quarterly.
  - Peffers et al. (2007), the DSR methodology, Journal of MIS.
  - Sein et al. (2011), "Action Design Research", MIS Quarterly. This is directly relevant to the expert study run inside an insurer.

  - hybrid intelligence (Dellermann, Ebel, Söllner and Leimeister, Business & Information Systems Engineering, 2019);

  - automation bias and complacency (Parasuraman and Manzey, Human Factors, 2010).

  These matter for the framing of Section 7.

  - the CMMN standard (OMG);

### M2. The tenancy regime is circular, and the synthetic data are aligned with the design's assumptions

**Where:** Section 6.1 (p. 11); Section 8, Limits (p. 19); Appendix E.4, "Known limitations" (p. 50); Appendix H.4 (p. 61).

**What:**
- The tenancy references "come from the engine CasePath implements" (p. 19), under the silence clause that CasePath encodes as presumptions. A design that executes the same engine on correctly read facts reproduces the reference by construction.
- The headline "It held 97% of the documents that should wait for an answer" (abstract) partly measures agreement with one's own semantics. "Should wait" is defined by exact deferral itself.
- The texts were generated so that presumptions are always correct: "A false one is mostly left unmentioned (139 facts)" and "Every true branch fact is stated" (p. 49). In real files, silence often means "not yet asked", which is the main risk of a silence presumption. Proposition 3.4 (p. 6) notes that "A presumption that the file's facts contradict is such a mistake", but the test data contain no such case, so the most important failure mode of presumptions is unmeasured.
- One model family wrote every text, and a model of the same family performed the blind check (p. 56).

**Why it matters:** The tenancy result answers "does the design apply a known rule system to explicit synthetic texts as its own engine would?". It does not answer "does it request the right evidence". The abstract and conclusion read as the latter.

**What would resolve it:**
1. Say in the abstract and the first page that the tenancy references are generated by the engine CasePath implements, under a silence policy all designs receive.
2. Report a sensitivity analysis in which a stated share of unmentioned facts are true (for example 5%, 10% or 20%), computed from the stored checklists by changing the reference only. This needs no model calls. It shows how fast the advantage of presumptions erodes, and whether comparators that also received the silence clause erode equally.
3. Report a fact-level table for CasePath's admitted values against the designed fact states in `fresh2/`, analogous to Table 20, which the authors already have for the blind reader: wrong admitted values, wrong presumptions, unresolved values. This would turn Proposition 3.4 from a definition into a measured property.
4. Move "One model wrote every synthetic text …" from the Limits paragraph into Section 6.1, where the data are introduced.

### M3. The tenancy effect comes from 33 author-designed gating claims, and the error unit amplifies them

**Where:** Section 6.4, "Where the tenancy difference lies" (p. 14); Table 21 (p. 51); Appendix E.4 (p. 49); Appendix D.3, "Before the run" (p. 45).

**What:**
- **The contrast comes from a minority of claims.** On the 33 denied- or open-event claims, CasePath made 0.47 strict unsafe errors per claim against 5.69 and 5.31. On the 63 ordinary claims it made 0.20 against 0.44 and 0.28, with no difference from the frontier model (p = 0.409). A third of the claims therefore carry H2 and H3.
- **These families are new in this test set.** They were added because "Only 16 such combinations remain with the defining event true" (p. 49). The development probe "was written with knowledge of the test set's design, its families and subsets" (p. 45). The plan's prediction that the difference lies there ("As the plan predicted", p. 2) is not independent of that knowledge.
- **The measure counts per document.** A design that misjudges one gating fact on a denied-event claim is charged with every document of the workflow: Rules in context has 5.68 strict errors per denied-event claim (Table 21). The measure rewards getting the gating fact right far more than everything else combined.
- **The gold standard on these claims is a modelling choice.** The denied-event claims are cases where "the landlord has announced a termination … but the tenant has received nothing in writing" (p. 49), and the reference requests nothing. Whether a legal-protection handler would request nothing when a termination has been announced orally is a modelling decision. The paper does not justify it from the insurer's or the law's perspective.
- **"Model executes rules" captures much of the gain without code.** With an instruction to enumerate completions, it reaches 0.83 overall and 1.94 on denied-event claims, against 5.68 for Rules in context (Table 21, p. 51). Much of the advantage may come from explicit semantics in the instructions rather than from executing them in code.

**Why it matters:** The headline numbers (0.31 against 2.14 and 1.86) are dominated by the test-set composition, which the authors chose, and by the unit of counting. A sceptical reader will see the abstract's numbers as an artefact of prevalence.

**What would resolve it:**
1. Report claim-level outcomes for every design, per subset: the share of claims with an exactly correct checklist, and the share with at least one unsafe error.
2. Report a reweighted overall estimate under stated prevalences of denied, open and ordinary claims; at least one should resemble the insurer's intake.
3. Give the abstract's tenancy result per subset, for example: "fewer errors where the claim denies or leaves open the event that defines the dispute (33 of 96 claims); no difference from the frontier model on the other 63".
4. Cite the source that makes "announced, not received in writing" a denied defining event with no documents needed. Show what the single prompts wrote on these claims: did they misread the denial, or decide that an announced termination warrants documents?
5. Discuss the "Model executes rules" result as evidence about how much of the effect comes from code rather than from explicit three-valued semantics.

### M4. The comparators were not developed with equal effort, and the reviewed rules favour execution

**Where:** Section 5 (pp. 10–11); Section 6.2 (p. 12); Section 8, "What an organisation must supply" (p. 19); Appendix H (p. 56); Table 23 (p. 52).

**What:**
- **The development effort was one-sided.** The product was developed over three studies, with development data, a probe, a rule review and settings chosen on development data (p. 45: "The default open rule … was set after a second independent review"). The baselines' texts are "unchanged since repository commit a062edd8" (p. 56), so the baselines received no comparable tuning.
- **The reviewed rules harm the in-context designs.** The rule review, made with knowledge of the test conditions (p. 19), helps execution and harms in-context use. The frontier single prompt makes 0.67 errors with the original rules and 0.95 with the reviewed ones, and leaves 91% against 45% of unresolved pairs unchanged (p. 19). In Study 2 the repaired workflow raised Rules in context with the frontier model "from 0.54 to 1.65 per pair" (p. 10).
- **The conversion choices also favour execution.** The theft workflow fixes "the cheapest route … at conversion" (p. 61), so the rules arrive in a form designed for code.
- **The paper leaves the key observation unexplained.** It calls the frontier result "One observation we leave unexplained" (p. 19). It is central: H1 and the would-be H5 are measured on rules that suit execution better than prompting.

**Why it matters:** "Fewer errors than the same model applying the same rules" is weaker when the rules were refined for, and with, one of the two designs. This is the first question a sceptical referee asks of any system-versus-baseline paper.

**What would resolve it:**
1. State in Section 6.2 how much development each baseline received, and whether any baseline prompt was ever tuned on development data.
2. Report H1's contrast also against the best single-prompt configuration on each regime: Rules in context with the original rules, which scores 1.47 on the cheap model and 0.67 on the frontier model. Present the original-rule rows in the main text, not only in Table 4 and Figure 4.
3. Offer an explanation, with evidence from the stored outputs, of why the reviewed rules hurt the in-context designs, for example prompt length or the representation of routes.
4. Add "the rules were reviewed with knowledge of the test conditions and in a format suited to execution" to the abstract-level qualification of H1.

### M5. The formal statements do not say what the text claims, and the system does not meet their premises

**Where:** Section 3 (pp. 4–6); Appendix A (pp. 22–23); Section 4.1 (p. 7).

**What:**
1. **Proposition 3.1 holds only in a fragment.** It is stated without qualification on p. 5. Its proof (p. 22) restricts it to "the flow fragment of the process engine", and the appendix concedes that outside it "Proposition 3.1 can fail". The scope belongs in the statement, together with the claim that every evaluated workflow lies in the fragment.
2. **The semantics assume independent Boolean facts.** Completions are taken over C → {T, F} (p. 5). The workflows contain enum- and value-typed facts (`claim_type` "enum", p. 61; the reading form, p. 57: "a value (a choice, a date or a text)") and logically related facts (`specialist_needed` and `deposit_considered` on p. 61). Infeasible completions can make exact deferral hold documents that every feasible completion needs. "Exact" is exact only for the unconstrained Boolean abstraction.
3. **The exactness has a cut-off.** "above eight, CasePath stops at the first open branch" (p. 6). Study 1 used a fallback at "twelve" to strong Kleene evaluation (p. 24). Prop. 3.3 therefore does not describe the product beyond that threshold. Say so, and reconcile the two thresholds.
4. **The admission rule differs between sections.**
   - p. 4: a value is admitted only "when every reading gives it", but "a reading that finds the presumed value supports it". A presumed fact can therefore be admitted with one supporting reading and two "not stated", a weaker standard than agreement.
   - p. 7 says that "readings that give different values leave the fact for a person, and a value only some readings give leaves it unresolved". It is unclear whether disagreement means "unresolved" (Section 3.2) or "handed to a person".
   - State one admission rule as a table of cases.
5. **Proposition 3.4 and Figure 6 are underspecified.**
   - Part (b) of Proposition 3.4 restates the definition of an unsafe error. The empirically relevant claim, that agreement makes wrong admitted values rare, is only argued: "a wrong value survives only if every reading gives it" (p. 6). Three samples from one model with one prompt are not independent readers, so systematic misreadings survive agreement.
   - The noise model behind Figure 6 says each fact is misread "with probability 0.1, either as unresolved or as the opposite value" (p. 23), but not how the 0.1 splits between the two.
   - The coincidence of 84.5 appearing three times (expected holds; misses under "false"; misses from wrong values) needs a sentence of explanation.
   - Figure 6's numbers (8.4, 3.7, 14.1) are apparently percentages of the 1002 documents, while the text gives counts.
6. **Models also decide document status.** "Rules That Decide" understates the role of models. Whether a document is "present" or "requested" is decided by the document finder and the second judgment, both model calls (p. 7, Table 2). In Study 1, missed documents came from exactly this sufficiency judgment (p. 32). The formalism treats "present" as given ("present if the file contains it", p. 5).

**What would resolve it:**
- Add the scope conditions (flow fragment, Boolean independent facts, number of open facts) to the proposition statements.
- Give the admission rule as a table of cases.
- Specify the noise model and the units in Figure 6.
- Report the measured fact-level error rate of admitted values (see M2), including how often all three readings agreed on a wrong value.
- State explicitly that document sufficiency is a model judgment outside the guarantees.

### M6. Statistical inference with 9 clusters, and a dependence the clustering ignores

**Where:** Section 6.3 (pp. 12–13); Table 5 (p. 13); Table 24 (p. 53); Table 27 (p. 54).

**What:**
- **The bootstrap interval contradicts the exact test.** Theft inference rests on 9 clusters (changed facts). Percentile cluster-bootstrap intervals with 9 clusters are known to under-cover. Table 5 shows it: H4's interval "−1.13 to −0.08" excludes zero, while the exact test gives p = 0.0625. Readers will see a contradiction, and the text does not explain it.
- **The bootstrap p-values are not credible at this resolution.** H5 "outside the procedure" is printed with a "bootstrap p" of 0.0001 (Table 5), and S4b with 0.0035 (Table 24). With 9 clusters there are at most 9^9 distinct resamples, and the bootstrap distribution is lumpy. The "share of resamples in which the difference reached the margin" (p. 40) is not a calibrated p-value at this sample size.
- **The clustering ignores the crossed design.** Theft is crossed: 8 stories × 9 facts. Clustering on facts treats the 8 stories as independent replicates. They are not: the paper itself shows that errors in a story's base text recur across its pairs ("Counting an error repeated across a story's pairs once", p. 16). The story is the natural unit of generalisation for "new texts".
- **The tenancy tests are driven by a few families.** The tests use magnitude-based sign-flip over 24 families, but 8 of those families hold the denied-event claims with differences of about 5 errors. The test is valid, but readers should see the family-level distribution.

**What would resolve it:**
1. Replace the percentile-bootstrap intervals for the theft contrasts with intervals obtained by inverting the sign-flip test, or caveat them in the caption.
2. Explain the H4 discrepancy in the text.
3. Remove the bootstrap non-inferiority p-values, or report them only as descriptive with a warning.
4. Report every theft hypothesis also with stories as clusters (8), and with two-way (story × fact) resampling as a sensitivity analysis.
5. Add a family-level dot plot of the tenancy differences, marked by subset, to the appendix.

### M7. Neither the checklist-only comparison nor the cost split supports "the agents after the readings changed no checklist"

**Where:** Abstract-level claims on p. 2 and p. 20; Section 6.4, "Which agents matter" (p. 15); Table 25 (p. 53); Appendix D.3 (p. 46).

**What:**
1. **The two runs are not independent.** "The two designs share the cached responses for routing and for part of the readings" (p. 15). Shared responses push the comparison towards "no difference".
2. **"No difference" is not equivalence.** The contrast was p = 0.5 and p = 0.594 with 7 and 6 informative clusters, and no margin was set.
3. **The evidence counts additions, not changed checklists.** "In no case-run did a need added by a later agent enter the scored checklist" shows that additions stayed suggestions. It does not show that the checklists were identical: the source checker can turn a "present" document into "unusable" (Table 2). The paper does not report the number of case-runs whose full and checklist-only checklists differ.
4. **The cost split is inconsistent.** Table 25 books the agents that "cannot decide" the checklist at USD 0.0055 of 0.0134 (41%) on tenancy and 0.0112 of 0.0300 (37%) on theft. Yet the checklist-only run costs 22% and 16%, and the conclusion says the later agents "cost most of a run". The two accounts must be reconciled; the brief, booked to the claim reader, appears to be the difference.
5. **The frontier "CasePath" is a different configuration.** The design called "CasePath on the frontier model" (pp. 2, 15, 18) is in fact "CasePath, checklist only, frontier model, one run" (Table 4). The text compares it with the full product on the cheap model ("0.31 against 0.30").

**Why it matters:** Design principle (4), "Spend calls only on agents that can change the checklist" (p. 19), is a strong practical recommendation. It rests on an absence of evidence, and it ignores the unevaluated value of the brief and the reply draft to handlers.

**What would resolve it:**
- Report the share of case-runs in which the two settings produced identical checklists.
- Run an equivalence test with a stated margin, or reword to "no detectable difference".
- Reconcile the cost accounting.
- Rename the frontier design consistently everywhere.
- Restate principle (4) as conditional on the brief and reply draft having no value, which has not been tested.

### M8. The integrity of the freeze and the independence of the test data from the developers need fuller disclosure

**Where:** Section 6.1 (pp. 11–12); "Irregularities" (p. 17); Appendix D.3 (pp. 44–46); Appendix G.4 (pp. 55–56).

**What:**
- **The tested commit may not be the released product.** "The run started before the product's owners confirmed the merge of the tested commit. If they reject a change of behaviour, the test must be repeated on new sealed data" (p. 45). The abstract says "We tested the released product", so the released and tested versions may differ. The paper does not say who "the product's owners" are, or how they relate to the authors.
- **The test texts were written by an agent with repository access.** "The texts of fresh2/ were written by Claude Opus 5.5, working as an agent in this repository" (p. 56). The paper does not say what that agent could read: the product's prompts and settings, the development results, the probe. Nor does it say whether the same agent sessions also implemented product changes ("AI coding and writing assistants were used to implement the harness and the product changes", p. 56). Independence of test-set authoring from system development is the core of a sealed test, and it is asserted only for the evaluated models ("no evaluated model read or wrote any of these data").
- **The timeline is compressed and the record is partly handwritten.**
  - `fresh/` was sealed on 9 November and `fresh2/` on 10 November (pp. 48, 50).
  - The confirmatory run took place from 10 November 22:30 to 11 November 04:17 (p. 44).
  - Four of five invocations stopped early, three of them unplanned.
  - Log corrections were "written by hand", and spending caps were changed on resumption (pp. 44–46).

  Nothing here invalidates the outputs, which are cached and complete, but the body gives only a four-line summary.
- **The open-rule setting is unexplained.** It was set after "a second independent review of the product overruled a criterion we had set earlier" (p. 45). The paper does not say who reviewed, which criterion was overruled, or when this happened relative to the sealing.

**What would resolve it:**
- Add a dated timeline table to Section 6.1 covering plan commits, data sealing, the probe, the open-rule decision, the product freeze, run start and end, and analysis.
- State exactly what the data-writing agent could access, and that it did not develop the product, or acknowledge the risk if it did.
- Confirm the merge status of the tested commit, and drop "released" from the abstract if it is not confirmed.
- Add the conflict-of-interest and role statements TMIS requires: the authors' relationship to the insurer and to the "product's owners".

### M9. The expert-study section should not be in the body in its current form

**Where:** Section 7 (pp. 17–18); contribution (4) (p. 2); Appendix I (pp. 62–65).

See also (h) and the answer to question 6 below.

**What:**
- Section 7 describes a study whose "sessions … take place after this version is submitted; their results will be added to it" (p. 17). A paper cannot be accepted on promised results.
- Contribution (4) and the abstract's "register a study with claims experts on real files" claim credit for work not done.
- The design has issues a reviewer of a registered report would raise:
  1. **The measured interface is not the tested one.** At the tested commit the interface "has no control for closing a case and none for a note" (p. 18). These are being built before the freeze, so the expert study will measure a different product from the one tested in Section 6.
  2. **The body and the appendix describe P differently.** The body says P is "the insurer's requests …, transcribed" (p. 17). Table 29 says P is made by "the product builder's automated labeller … checked by the study lead" (p. 62). The source of the primary reference is therefore an automated labeller built by the product's developer, and the body does not say so.
  3. **The study cases were seen in development.** The product was developed on them (p. 17), which the authors acknowledge.
  4. **Experts may have handled these files before.** "Whether an expert handled a case before is taken from the file's metadata" (p. 63), so experts may have produced P themselves.
  5. **No ethics approval is mentioned.** The paper states consent by the insurer and by each expert only. TMIS will expect an ethics statement.
  6. **The single test is weak.** It is a one-sided sign test over five experts, with a floor of 1/32.

**What would resolve it:** Remove Section 7 from the body and contribution (4). Keep at most one paragraph under future work that points to the pre-registration (Appendix I or the repository), corrected for the P discrepancy. Alternatively, hold the paper until the study has run and report it fully.

## (d) Minor concerns

1. **Terms used before they are defined.**
   - The abstract and introduction use "Rules in context", "Self-consistency", "Model executes rules", "strict unsafe errors", "wrong + missed changes per pair", "cost-efficient model" and "frontier model" before any definition. The first definitions are in Table 4 (p. 12) and Section 6.2.
   - Give a one-clause gloss at first use, or define the two measures in words in the abstract.
2. **Two names for one study.** The body calls the confirmatory test "Section 6"; the appendices call it "Study 3" (D.3, E.4, F.3, G.2). Introduce the name "Study 3" in Section 6.

## (e) Claims against evidence

Each item gives the quoted sentence, then what the results support.

**Abstract (p. 1)**

1. *"A language model that applies an organisation's rules in one prompt answers for each document and gives no account of the facts behind the answer."*
   - The Rules in context prompt asks for "a short reason and exact citations" (p. 37), and Model executes rules returns each condition's value (p. 59).
   - Supported: "… gives no account of the facts that code could check."
2. *"Code then executes the organisation's rules and holds exactly the requests that depend on a fact still open."*
   - Exactness holds relative to the admitted values, within the flow fragment, for independent Boolean facts and up to eight open facts. Document presence is a model judgment.
   - Supported: "holds exactly the requests whose need, given the admitted facts, depends on an open fact".
3. *"We tested the released product on sealed synthetic claims …"*
   - The merge of the tested commit was unconfirmed at run time (p. 45).
   - Supported: "the product at a frozen commit".
4. *"… and fewer tenancy errors than a frontier model (0.31 against 1.86), chiefly where a rule stops or holds the whole checklist."*
   - On the 63 ordinary claims there was no difference (p = 0.409). The references are engine-generated.
   - Supported: "… on the 33 claims whose defining event is denied or open (0.47 against 5.31); no difference on the other 63; references generated by the engine CasePath implements".
5. *"It held 97% of the documents that should wait for an answer, against 48% and 35%, at 2.1 and 7.4 times the frontier single prompt's cost."*
   - 97% is tenancy only. "Should wait" is defined by the design's own semantics. The 7.4 is a theft cost ratio. CasePath also held twice as many unneeded documents (0.33 against 0.17 and 0.18).
   - Supported: "On tenancy it held 97% of the documents the engine-generated reference holds (48% and 35%), while holding more documents the reference does not (0.33 against 0.17–0.18 per claim); it cost 2.1 times the frontier single prompt on tenancy and 7.4 times on theft."
6. *"We release the product, workflows, data and analysis, and register a study with claims experts on real files."*
   - The insurer's workflows and Knowledge are not released (p. 55). The study has not run.
   - Supported: "We release the code, the research workflows, the synthetic data and the analysis."

**Introduction (pp. 1–2)**

7. *"A fact the file does not state, a fact it states false and a fact it leaves open can then lead to the same answer, and Section 3 shows what each conflation costs."*
   - Section 3 shows the costs of two-valued semantics under a synthetic noise model, not of single prompts.
   - Supported: "Section 3 shows what conflating them costs a rule engine."
8. *"… and code admits a value only when the readings agree and the quotation is in the file."*
   - Presumed values need only one supporting reading, or none (p. 4).
   - Supported: add "; unstated facts take a presumed value unless a reading quotes doubt".
9. *"As the plan predicted, the tenancy difference lies in the 33 claims whose defining event is denied or left open."*
   - The plan was written with knowledge of the subsets the authors had designed, and after a probe built for them. A difference on ordinary claims was also found against the same model (p = 0.0417, descriptive).
   - Supported: "Most of the tenancy difference lies in the 33 claims … which the test set was designed to include."
10. *"The agents after the readings added cost and changed no checklist …"*
    - What was measured is that no need added by a later agent entered a scored checklist, and that there was no detectable difference in errors between runs that shared cached responses. Checklist identity was not reported.
    - Supported: "… none of their additions entered a scored checklist, and dropping them showed no detectable difference in errors".
11. *"Two earlier pre-registered studies found execution no better than Rules in context and traced each shortfall to a cause …"*
    - The attributions were post hoc, partly by manual inspection (p. 44), and the fixes' joint effect was measured only on development data (p. 11).
    - Supported: "… and attributed each shortfall, post hoc, to a likely cause".
12. *"(4) A registered study in which claims experts review CasePath's checklists on real cases (Section 7)."*
    - Not a result. Remove it from the contributions.
13. *"(2) CasePath, released with … a parity check of deployed builds."*
    - The parity check establishes code identity only (p. 10).

**Discussion (pp. 18–19)**

14. *"It pays where many rules are in force: … while on ShARC, with one short rule per item, the two-reading core was no better than prompting."*
    - This is a cross-dataset, cross-design comparison, with no manipulation of the number of rules.
    - Supported: "We conjecture that it pays where many rules are in force; the ShARC result is consistent with this but does not test it."
15. *"And it pays most for the cost-efficient model: inside CasePath the two models showed no difference in tenancy errors (0.31 against 0.30 …)."*
    - Equal performance inside CasePath does not show where the gain is largest. The frontier configuration was checklist-only. On tenancy the gain over the frontier single prompt is similar for both models (S7b: 0.30 against 1.86).
    - Supported: "On theft, the gain from execution was larger with the cost-efficient model (−1.15) than with the frontier model (−0.12, p = 0.672); on tenancy it was similar."
16. *"The agents after the readings changed no scored checklist (Section 6.4); they buy only the brief, the reply draft and the checks a handler reads."*
    - The value of these outputs was not measured, so "only" is unsupported. "Changed no scored checklist" was not measured either (see 10).
17. *Design principle (1): "Execute in code every rule that can stop or hold the checklist; that is where the tenancy difference lay."*
    - Model executes rules, without code, closed much of the gap (Table 21).
    - Supported: "… or at least state its semantics explicitly; code removed most of the remaining errors."
18. *Design principle (3): "… in development, a second reading cut theft errors where a checking call had not (Table 3)."*
    - The Table 3 evidence comes from pairing other *runs'* readings in a recomputation (p. 42: "because it used the other runs' readings"), not from a second reading in one run.
19. *Design principle (4): "Spend calls only on agents that can change the checklist; the checklist-only setting showed no difference from the full product."*
    - Absence of a detected difference is not equivalence; the comparison is cache-confounded; and the other agents' value to handlers is untested.

**Conclusion (pp. 19–20)**

20. *"… it made fewer errors than the same cost-efficient model applying the rules in one prompt, on theft and tenancy, and fewer than a frontier model on tenancy."*
    - Add: "on synthetic claims with engine-generated tenancy references, mainly on claims whose defining event is denied or open".
21. *"It held what waits on an open fact, which the single prompts did less often."*
    - Add: "and held more documents that did not need to wait".
22. *"The agents after the readings cost most of a run and changed no checklist."*
    - Table 25 books the non-deciding agents at 41% and 37% of cost; see M7.
23. *"For a practitioner: the model reads, the organisation's rules decide, and what the file does not settle becomes a question."*
    - Models also decide document presence and sufficiency, and presumptions decide unstated facts. The practitioner evidence is synthetic.

**Section 2 and Table 1 (pp. 2–4)**

## (h) What I liked

- **Pre-registration with teeth.** The data loader refuses to run unless the plan is committed unmodified, and refuses in a process with a model client loaded (p. 50). A fixed-sequence procedure is applied honestly: H4 failed, and H5 was then *not* tested, even though its point estimate would have looked good.
- **Failures reported as results.** Two failed earlier studies are reported, with numbers. The paper says plainly that the rule review was done by AI sessions and no jurist (p. 11).
- **A concrete formal example.** Proposition 3.2's reconvergence example (p. 5, Figure 2) is crisp, and it shows a real defect of strong Kleene evaluation in a deployed engine, not a toy one.
- **Code-enforced permissions.** Table 2 states what each agent may never change, as the code enforces it. This is the kind of governance artefact an IS audience values.
- **The two-valued ablation.** Re-executing the same readings with two-valued semantics isolates the contribution of the third value without new calls.
- **Thorough irregularity reporting.** It covers handwritten log corrections, cache sharing between designs, and every departure from the plan's text (pp. 44–46). It is unusually complete; I would like it in the body (M8).
- **Strong release package.** The cached responses, ledgers and the byte-for-byte reproduction command for the analysis (p. 55) make the quantitative claims checkable without new spending.
- **Careful thinking about references in the expert-study design.** It refuses to call unsettled items right or wrong, keeps the status sources apart, and treats consensus formed under exposure as a lower bound. Once results exist, this deserves a paper of its own.

## Answers to the specific questions in the assignment

**6. Is it appropriate to include a registered design without results?**

No, not in the body of a journal article under review. It inflates the contribution list, occupies two pages and a table, and invites acceptance on promise. The design also has a discrepancy over the source of P, and an interface mismatch with the tested commit (M9).

I would ask the authors to do one of two things:
- remove Section 7 and contribution (4), deposit the pre-registration publicly with a timestamp, and cite it in one sentence of future work; or
- hold this submission until the study has run, and report it as a second evaluation in a revised version.

If the editor prefers to keep it, it should become a short appendix labelled "registered protocol, no results". The body should state the P-labeller provenance, the ethics approval, and the experts' prior exposure to the cases.

**7. Reproducibility and release as described.**

The re-scoring path is good. Stored outputs and caches reproduce every number without model calls, and the run command checks the plan, commit, parity hash and workflow hashes (pp. 54–55).

What is missing or weak:
- **Model identification:** provider, snapshot, routing, temperature and reasoning effort (minor 8).
- **Insurer materials:** the insurer's workflows and Knowledge are not released, which is understandable, but the abstract says "We release the product, workflows".
- **Cached reproduction versus re-run:** a re-run with an empty cache will differ because the models are nondeterministic. Report run-to-run variation as part of the result, not only as Jaccard agreement.
- **Model retirement:** the paper depends on two pseudonymous model endpoints. If they are retired, only the cached reproduction remains, and the paper should say so.
- **Parity hash scope:** it covers the source tree; the paper is clear about this. Contribution (2) should not overstate it.
